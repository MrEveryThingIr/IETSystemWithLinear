> This chapter describes a hypothesis, not a finished feature. Everything in it is something we want experts to test, correct or reject.

## Start from needs, not from prices

In an ordinary market, price is the language everyone must speak. When prices move quickly and unevenly, the language itself becomes unreliable: the same hour of plastering buys less bread every week, and nobody can plan.

Our starting point is simpler. Every person has things they need and things they can offer. A *need* is "a plastered wall". An *offer* is "I can plaster walls". A match is a pair that fits.

## Why a circuit?

Pairs rarely fit perfectly. The plasterer needs dental care, the dentist needs a delivery, the courier needs a plastered wall. Economists call the missing fit the *double coincidence of wants*. But a **loop** can fit when a pair cannot: plasterer → dentist → courier → plasterer.

Think of an electrical circuit. Current flows only when the loop is closed, and at every junction what flows in equals what flows out. Applied to people:

* each person is a junction;
* what they give and what they receive, in a value they have agreed on, are both written down;
* a closed loop means nobody is left holding something they cannot use;
* an open loop is visible as a balance: someone has given more than they have received, and the system shows exactly who and how much.

If every agreement inside a loop is made in terms of work, goods and time that the people themselves defined, then a rise in the general price level changes the numbers on paper but not whether each person's need is met. That is the hope.

## What exists today

* Needs and offers are recorded and **matched** with an explanation of why they fit.
* Agreements are versioned and accepted explicitly by every side.
* Work is reported and reviewed.
* Money, credit, advances, returns and **settlement in kind** (paying with goods or services at an agreed value) are recorded, and each balance is visible to the people involved.

What does **not** exist yet is automatic discovery of multi-person loops, or any built-in way to value a service against a good.

## The hard problems, stated plainly

We know the idea has precedent and pitfalls. Mutual-credit and barter networks (LETS, WIR in Switzerland, Sardex in Sardinia, time banks) have run for decades, some successfully and some not. We do not claim to have solved what they struggled with:

1. **Valuation.** How do you agree that an hour of plastering equals a month of dental check-ups? Fixed rates? Negotiation each time? Hours of work? A basket of goods?
2. **Unit of account.** Something must say "how much". Is that the national currency, hours, a basket, or something new? Each choice has consequences when inflation is high.
3. **Credit and risk.** In an open loop someone is temporarily owed. Who decides how much of that is acceptable, and what happens if a participant disappears?
4. **Liquidity and fairness.** People with scarce skills can do very well; people with common skills may wait. How do we avoid a system that serves only the lucky?
5. **Trust and fraud.** Fake offers, collusion, over-claimed work.
6. **Law and tax.** Barter is often taxable and sometimes regulated. We must obey the law of the places where people use the system.
7. **Scale.** A loop of three friends is easy. A loop of three hundred strangers is not.

## What we are *not* doing

* We are not issuing a coin or token. Whether any internal unit is even needed is an open question.
* We are not asking anyone to invest.
* We are not promising that this protects anyone from inflation. We are trying to find out, carefully, whether and how it can.
